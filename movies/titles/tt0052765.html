The genetics

Jean Renoir's comedy is a satire of scientific rationalism, and its target is eugenics. Étienne Alexis wants to replace sexual reproduction with state-run artificial insemination, selecting donors to breed a better population and, he hopes, to do away with passion altogether. Genetics as such is never discussed in technical terms; what the film mocks is the idea that reproduction can be planned rationally from above. The scientist is a respected public figure and a presidential candidate, presented as stiff and well-meaning rather than evil, and the film's answer to him is nature itself: a wind, a flute and a summer afternoon are enough to make him abandon his programme and father a child the old-fashioned way. Made in 1959, the film treats eugenic ambitions as absurd rather than menacing, and links them to a technocratic vision of the new united Europe.

The story

Étienne Alexis, a celebrated French biologist, is the favourite to become the first president of a united Europe. His programme calls for compulsory artificial insemination to improve humanity and make it worthy of modern science. To celebrate his engagement to his German cousin Marie-Charlotte, he holds a picnic near his estate in Provence, beside the ruins of a temple of Diana. Among the servants is Nénette, a farmer's daughter who wants a child without a husband and has volunteered for his insemination experiments. When the goatherd Gaspard plays his flute, a sudden wind scatters the party, a bacchanal breaks out, and Étienne, separated from the others, falls for Nénette. After a few days at her father's house he is dragged back to his old life, but on his wedding day he finds Nénette pregnant with his child and decides to marry her instead.
