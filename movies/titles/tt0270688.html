The genetics

Cloning is the premise of this playful feminist science fiction comedy by Lynn Hershman Leeson. A scientist creates copies of herself by injecting her DNA into artificial beings, for companionship and to do chores, and finds she cannot control them. The clones' need for the Y chromosome, which they obtain by collecting sperm, is a satirical conceit rather than biology, and the story mixes genetics with computer viruses: the men they sleep with are infected by a virus that also crashes their computers. Rosetta is an awkward, lonely but sympathetic scientist, neither villain nor hero, and the clones are presented as a family seeking autonomy. German critics saw it as a playful contribution to the cloning debate. Real cloning does not produce adult copies, and nothing in genetics requires a supply of Y chromosomes.

The story

Bio-geneticist Rosetta Stone, a lonely scientist who works on the artificial production of organs, uses her own DNA to create three female clones, Ruby, Olive and Marinne, self-replicating automatons who live in a cyberspace inside her computer and whom she calls her family. To survive they need a constant supply of the Y chromosome, that is, semen. Ruby, who has learned how men and women behave from classic romance films, goes out at night, seduces men and brings their sperm back for her sisters. The men become impotent, a barcode appears on their foreheads and their computers crash, and FBI agent Hopper investigates. Rosetta loses control of her rebellious creations and is arrested by mistake. In the end the clones gain their independence, Marinne makes new clones, Rosetta finds love with Hopper, and Ruby falls for her shy neighbour Sandy and becomes pregnant.
