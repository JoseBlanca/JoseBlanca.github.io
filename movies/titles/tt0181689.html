The genetics

Genetics plays a modest but important role in the film's backstory. The precogs' clairvoyance is a side effect of damage caused in the womb by their mothers' addiction to neuroin, and Dr. Iris Hineman, a geneticist, studied these children and developed the research on which Precrime is based. She is portrayed as an ambiguous, reclusive figure living apart from the system her work made possible, and it is she who reveals that its supposed infallibility depends on deleting dissenting visions. The film is less about heredity than about determinism: whether a person can be punished for a predicted act and whether the future is fixed. In that sense it touches the same concerns raised by genetic prediction of behaviour and disease risk, though the film's mechanism is psychic rather than genetic. The precogs themselves are treated as tools of the state and are only freed, to live in anonymity, at the end.

The story

In 2054, Washington, D.C.'s experimental Precrime unit arrests murderers before they act, using the visions of three "precogs" who foresee homicides. Its chief, John Anderton, still grieving the abduction of his son, is himself predicted to kill a man he has never met, Leo Crow. On the run, he visits Dr. Iris Hineman, the geneticist whose research made Precrime possible. She tells him that the precogs' abilities arose in children born to mothers addicted to the drug neuroin, and that the precogs sometimes disagree: the dissenting vision, a "minority report", is deleted. Anderton kidnaps the strongest precog, Agatha, to search her memories, and uncovers a murder covered up by Precrime's director, Lamar Burgess: the killing of Agatha's own mother, Anne Lively, who had tried to reclaim her. Exposed, Burgess kills himself, Precrime is shut down and its prisoners are released.
