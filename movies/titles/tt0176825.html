The genetics

Genetics, in its historical guise as eugenics, is the entire subject of this documentary. It distinguishes positive eugenics, which sought to "improve" humans through selective mating, from negative eugenics, which aimed to prevent those judged inferior from reproducing, and shows how both were promoted by physicians, biologists and state institutions as science-based social policy. Scientists appear as central actors: Galton, Davenport, the Swedish race biologists and Eugen Fischer's German institute, which treated genetics as a subordinate tool of racial hygiene. The film stresses that eugenics was not confined to Nazi Germany but had broad support in democratic countries, including Sweden's welfare state, and that racial ideas of Nordic superiority linked the Swedish and German programmes. It is a useful record of how early human genetics became entangled with racism and coercive state policies.

The story

This Swedish documentary by Peter Cohen traces eugenics, which Francis Galton founded on the idea that human evolution was being held back by the breeding of the "unfit", through its application in several countries in the first half of the twentieth century. In the United States, it shows the 1916 film The Black Stork, in which Dr. Harry Haiselden plays himself refusing to save a disabled newborn, a case based on his real practice, and describes Charles Davenport's Eugenics Record Office and the first compulsory sterilisation laws. In Sweden, it covers the State Institute for Race Biology, founded in 1922, and the sterilisation law of 1934. It discusses the Soviet Union, where Mendelian heredity clashed with ideology and scientists collected brains such as Lenin's, and Nazi Germany, where the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Anthropology, Human Heredity and Eugenics helped lead to forced sterilisation and the killing of disabled people.
