The genetics

Genetics appears as a tool of virology and public health rather than as a theme in itself. Scientists identify the new virus by its genetic material, showing that it is a recombinant of bat and pig viruses, model how its mutations increase transmission and use that knowledge to make a vaccine. The film portrays scientists and public health workers in an unusually positive and realistic light: they are competent, overworked and willing to take personal risks, as when Hextall tests the vaccine on herself, while the obstacles come from bureaucracy, fear, misinformation and inequality of access. Made with advice from the virologist W. Ian Lipkin and the epidemiologist Larry Brilliant, it was praised by scientists for its accuracy. The chain of infection from a bat colony disturbed by deforestation, through pigs, to humans echoes the Nipah virus, which emerged in Malaysia in the late 1990s, and the film even uses a Nipah protein model on screen. Its relevance was underlined in 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic brought it renewed popularity.

The story

Back in Minneapolis from Hong Kong, Beth Emhoff dies of an unknown disease, followed by her young son; her husband Mitch is immune. As the virus spreads worldwide, the film follows several interlinked stories. At the CDC, Dr. Ellis Cheever sends epidemiologist Dr. Erin Mears to trace Beth's contacts, and Mears herself is infected and dies. Dr. Ally Hextall finds that the new virus, MEV-1, combines genetic material from pig and bat viruses; when a researcher in San Francisco defies orders to destroy his samples and finds a way to grow it in cell culture, she develops a vaccine and tests it on herself. A WHO epidemiologist traces the outbreak to Macau and is kidnapped by an official who wants vaccines for his village, and a conspiracy blogger promotes a fake cure, while society breaks down until the vaccine is distributed by lottery. A final flashback reveals how the virus jumped from a bat to a pig and to Beth.
