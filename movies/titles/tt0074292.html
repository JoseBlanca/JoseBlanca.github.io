The genetics

The genetic element, in the sense used by this database, is a laboratory-held pathogen: a plague strain, vaguely identified as pneumonic, kept by the US military at an international health agency in Geneva as a germ warfare agent. Its accidental release on a train sets off the story, and the real villainy lies in the military's efforts to hide its bioweapons, to the point of sending hundreds of passengers to their deaths over a condemned bridge. The scientists come out better than the soldiers: Dr. Stradner suspects the truth and monitors the infected dog's recovery, and the neurologist Chamberlain reasons that the disease is less lethal than claimed. No genetic engineering is described. Released in the 1970s, the film reflects suspicion of secret military biological weapons research, which the 1972 Biological Weapons Convention had just banned.

The story

Terrorists attack the US mission at the International Health Organization in Geneva, where a plague strain is stored; one of them, Eklund, is exposed to it, escapes and boards a train from Geneva to Stockholm. Dr. Elena Stradner suspects the strain is a biological weapon, while US military intelligence Colonel Mackenzie insists it was being destroyed. Fearing all passengers are infected, Mackenzie seals the train and reroutes it towards a former Nazi concentration camp in Poland for quarantine, over the Cassandra Crossing, a decrepit bridge he knows may collapse; its fall would bury the fact that the US kept germ warfare agents in a neutral country. Aboard, the neurologist Jonathan Chamberlain notices that the disease is less deadly than claimed, and an infected dog recovers in the lab, but Mackenzie does not relent. Passengers fight the armed guards and uncouple the rear of the train; the front cars plunge with the bridge, and the survivors walk away.
