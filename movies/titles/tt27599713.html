The genetics

Like My Daughter Hildegart (1977), this film dramatises the real case of Aurora Rodríguez Carballeira, a feminist and eugenicist who conceived her daughter deliberately, choosing a father who would never claim paternity, in order to produce the "woman of the future". Genetics enters as eugenic intention rather than science: Aurora treats conception and upbringing as a single project of human improvement, the same logic she had earlier applied to her nephew, the child prodigy Pepito Arriola. The film concentrates on control, education and the daughter's struggle for independence, and it ends with the murder, which Aurora justified by comparing herself to a sculptor who destroys a flawed work. No scientists appear. The real Hildegart wrote on contraception, sexual reform and eugenics and was active in the Spanish sex-reform movement, which in the 1930s mixed progressive causes with eugenic ideas; the film evokes this milieu but uses it mainly as background for a mother–daughter drama.

The story

Aurora Rodríguez, an affluent woman, conceives a daughter with a clergyman and raises Hildegart at home as a prodigy destined to change the world. In Madrid in 1931, as the Second Spanish Republic is proclaimed, the teenage Hildegart publishes on female sexuality, joins the socialists, corresponds with Havelock Ellis and becomes a celebrity, always under her mother's surveillance. She falls in love with the activist Abel Vilella, and Aurora schemes to separate them, blackmailing the maid Macarena into framing Abel for crimes committed by her husband. Hildegart uncovers the plot, clears Abel and, with the journalist Eduardo de Guzmán's help, prepares to leave for London with him. After she tells her mother that she belongs to no one, Aurora concludes that "Project Hildegart" has failed and shoots her daughter in her sleep.
