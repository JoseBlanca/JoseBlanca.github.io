The genetics

The film's twist is human cloning used to replace the dead. Bruce Carter, unable to accept the death of his wife and daughter in a car crash, runs an automated laboratory in his barn that grows physical copies of them in incubation units, into which he downloads his wife's digitised consciousness. The copies are imperfect: they suffer identity rejection, seizures and memory glitches, and Bruce kills and buries each failed iteration, numbering them as experiments; he even plans a clone of himself without his painful memories. The film presents him as a man who does evil while believing himself justified, and the clones as victims who finally turn on him. The ending, with another copy emerging from the lab, keeps the horror open. Real cloning would produce a genetically identical baby, not an adult copy, and memories and personality cannot be transferred; the film uses the idea to explore grief, identity and control.

The story

In this science-fiction horror film, a badly injured woman wakes in a remote farmhouse next to a man, Bruce, who says he is her husband and that the girl Alice is their daughter. She believes she is someone else, Sarah Thompson, but Bruce blames brain damage from an accident. She finds a surgical wound on her neck, a living figure wrapped in plastic and the same wound on Alice. When Bruce tries to kill her, she flees to his barn and discovers a laboratory of cloning tanks: the real Ellie and Alice died years ago, and Bruce has been printing copies of them and loading Ellie's digitised mind into each one. She is clone number 163; the Sarah persona is a data glitch, and failed copies break down. Bruce kills and buries her, but she crawls out of the grave and overwrites the memories of the next clone with her own. The new Ellie plays along, then kills Bruce and escapes with Alice; behind them, another clone emerges.
