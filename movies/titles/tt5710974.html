The genetics

DNA is the episode's plot device: Daly creates conscious digital copies of real people from genetic material taken from a coffee cup or a child's lollipop, and the copies retain their originals' memories and personalities. The episode uses this to ask whether such copies are people and what is owed to them, and to portray abuse of power: as long as Daly keeps the samples, he can recreate and punish his victims at will. Daly is not a geneticist but a programmer who misuses biological data, and his downfall comes when his creations turn his own tools against him. The premise is scientifically impossible, a point several critics raised: a genome contains no memories, and DNA alone cannot reproduce a person's mind; an early draft explained the memories with a recording implant, an idea Charlie Brooker dropped as too much detail.

The story

Robert Daly, the brilliant but socially awkward co-founder and chief technical officer of the company that makes the virtual reality game Infinity, is ignored and belittled by his colleagues. In secret, he runs a modified version of the game based on his favourite TV show, Space Fleet, in which he is the heroic captain of the starship USS Callister and his crew are sentient digital clones of his co-workers, created from DNA he has collected from objects such as a discarded coffee cup. Inside the game, he humiliates and tortures them. When he clones the new programmer Nanette Cole, she refuses to submit and organises an escape: the crew blackmail the real Nanette into stealing the DNA samples from Daly's apartment, then steer the ship into a wormhole leading to the game's online update, hoping to be deleted. Instead they survive in the public version of the game, free, while Daly is trapped in his own build as it shuts down.
