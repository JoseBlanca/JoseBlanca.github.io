The genetics

The genetic theme is inherited madness. Adapted from Émile Zola's naturalist novel, the film presents Lantier as a man whose 'tainted blood subjects him to fits of homicidal mania', in the words of The New York Times review of 1938. His violence is a fate he did not choose and cannot escape, and the film sides with nature: no love, work or effort can free him, and the story ends with him killing the woman he loves and then himself. No science or scientist appears. Renoir compared Lantier to the doomed heroes of Greek tragedy. The idea of hereditary taint and degeneration was widespread in Zola's time; modern genetics does not support the notion of a family flaw that passes a compulsion to murder from generation to generation, although some psychiatric conditions do have a partly genetic basis.

The story

Jacques Lantier, a locomotive driver on the Paris to Le Havre line, loves his engine and suffers from headaches and fits of violent rage, worse when he drinks, that seize him when he is close to a woman. Visiting his aunt's village, he almost strangles Flore, a young woman he has known since childhood. On a train, he crosses the path of a murderer: the deputy stationmaster Roubaud, jealous of his wife Séverine's past with her rich godfather Grandmorin, has stabbed him to death with her complicity. Séverine asks Lantier to keep silent and begins an affair with him, then urges him to kill her husband. Lantier cannot do it, but when she says she will leave Roubaud, a fit overtakes him and he kills Séverine instead. The next day he confesses to his fireman and throws himself from the moving train.
