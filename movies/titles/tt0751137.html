The genetics

Inbreeding is the core of the episode. The baby's malformations are explained by generations of incest within the Peacock family, and laboratory tests establish that both of the baby's parents were Peacocks, which points the investigation towards the incest. Scully raises inbreeding as the medical explanation for the defects, while Mulder at first rejects it because the household seems to be all male. There are no scientists in the story; the genetic theme is presented as a horror of rural isolation and of a family determined to perpetuate itself at any cost. Real inbreeding does increase the risk of birth defects, because children of closely related parents are more likely to inherit two copies of a harmful recessive allele, but the episode exaggerates the effects for horror, and the family's claim that none of them can feel pain is not explained. The episode was inspired partly by the documentary Brother's Keeper and was controversial for its graphic content.

The story

In the small town of Home, Pennsylvania, children playing baseball find the body of a severely deformed newborn buried in a field. Mulder and Scully learn from the local sheriff that the nearby farmhouse belongs to the Peacock brothers, three deformed men whose family has lived there without electricity or running water and is rumoured to have been inbreeding since the Civil War. The autopsy shows the baby was buried alive, and laboratory tests show its parents were Peacocks. Believing the brothers have abducted and raped a woman, the agents investigate; the brothers murder the sheriff and his wife and kill a deputy with a booby trap. Searching the house, Mulder and Scully find Mrs. Peacock, the brothers' mother, a quadruple amputee hidden under a bed, who has continued to bear children with her sons. Two brothers die in the confrontation, but the mother and her eldest son escape to start a new family elsewhere.
