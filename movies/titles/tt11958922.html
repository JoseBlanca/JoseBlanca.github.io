The genetics

DNA testing is the main tool behind many of the exonerations described, though the series is about the failures of the justice system rather than genetics itself. The contrast between unreliable forensic methods and DNA evidence is clearest in the bite-mark cases. At Kennedy Brewer's 1995 trial, which ended in a death sentence, forensic odontologist Michael West testified that marks on the victim were "indeed and without a doubt" made by Brewer. A DNA test in 2001 excluded him, and the unknown DNA profile was later matched to Justin Albert Johnson, one of the original suspects, who confessed. Brewer was exonerated in 2008, and Brooks, convicted of a similar murder largely on bite-mark evidence, was also cleared. A 2009 National Academy of Sciences report found no scientific basis for identifying individuals by bite marks. The Innocence Project also notes that nearly 70% of the wrongful convictions overturned by DNA evidence involved mistaken eyewitness identification, the subject of the second part. Geneticists do not appear on screen; the scientists portrayed are forensic experts whose overconfident testimony caused harm.

The story

A nine-episode Netflix documentary series about wrongful convictions, based on the work of the Innocence Project, which seeks to exonerate people it believes were wrongly convicted. Directed and executive produced by Liz Garbus, Alex Gibney and Roger Ross Williams, among others, it is divided into three parts. "The Evidence" examines convictions based on discredited forensic testimony, especially bite-mark analysis, including the cases of Levon Brooks and Kennedy Brewer, convicted in Mississippi in the 1990s of murdering two young girls, and Keith Harward. "The Witness" deals with mistaken eyewitness identification, with the cases of Franky Carrillo and Thomas Haynesworth. "The Prosecution" turns to misconduct by police and prosecutors, following Chester Hollman III, Kenneth Wyniemko and Alfred Dewayne Brown, who was exonerated from death row in 2019.
