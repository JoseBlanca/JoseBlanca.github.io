The genetics

The film turns on the idea that evil can be inherited. Christine fears that her descent from a murderer is the 'bad seed' behind Rhoda's lack of conscience; her father answers that such behaviour is shaped mainly by nurture, not nature. The story sides with nature: Rhoda, raised by loving, respectable parents, is a remorseless killer, and the hidden grandmother is offered as the explanation. No scientist or test appears; heredity is a family secret revealed by an adoption. The ending, in which lightning kills Rhoda, was imposed by the Production Code and punishes the child rather than settling the debate. Current genetics does not support this picture. Twin and adoption studies show that antisocial and aggressive behaviour is partly heritable, but the genetic influence is spread over many genes of tiny effect and depends strongly on the environment a child grows up in. Nothing like a single inherited 'murder' trait passes intact from grandmother to granddaughter, and a family history of violence raises a child's risk only modestly; it does not make a child a killer.

The story

While her husband, an army colonel, is away in Washington, Christine Penmark grows uneasy about her prim, polite eight-year-old daughter Rhoda. After Rhoda loses a school penmanship medal to her classmate Claude, the boy drowns at a school picnic, and Christine finds the medal hidden among Rhoda's things. Pressing her father, the writer Richard Bravo, Christine learns that she was adopted as a toddler and is the biological daughter of a notorious serial killer. Rhoda admits that she struck Claude with her tap shoes and that she had already killed an elderly neighbour; when the janitor Leroy guesses the truth, she burns him to death. In despair, Christine gives Rhoda an overdose of sleeping pills and shoots herself. Both survive, but Rhoda is killed by lightning while trying to recover the medal from the lake.
