The genetics

Human cloning drives the whole plot. The film imagines a mature cloning industry built on pet cloning ("RePet") and a secret capacity to clone adult humans in hours, complete with their memories. Its scientist, Dr. Weir, is a sympathetic but compromised figure: he serves Drucker's illegal programme because he hopes to save his wife, whom he keeps recreating as she dies of cystic fibrosis. Drucker, the businessman, is the true villain: he uses cloning to cheat death and keep control of his fortune, and has his clones engineered with deliberately shortened lifespans as insurance against betrayal. The film raises questions about identity, legal personhood and ownership of clones, which have no legal rights in its world. The science is fictional: real cloning produces an embryo that must develop and grow like any other, so an adult copy with the original's memories is impossible. Pet cloning, however, has been offered as a service since the 2010s, so the film's opening premise is less far-fetched than it seemed in 2000.

The story

In the near future, animal cloning is commonplace, but human cloning is banned by "Sixth Day" laws. Charter pilot Adam Gibson is hired by Michael Drucker, billionaire owner of the cloning company Replacement Technologies, and undergoes what seem to be routine tests. That day, his partner Hank takes his place on the flight, and Drucker and Hank are killed by an anti-cloning assassin. Believing Adam was killed too, Drucker's people clone him, and Adam comes home to find a copy of himself living with his family. Hunted by Drucker's security team, he learns from the company's chief scientist, Dr. Griffin Weir, that his DNA and memories were recorded during the tests, that Drucker himself is a clone, and that Weir has repeatedly cloned his own wife, who is dying of cystic fibrosis. Adam and his clone join forces, destroy the facility and save the family; the clone starts a new life in Argentina.
