The genetics

This adaptation of Aldous Huxley's 1932 novel depicts a eugenic state built on reproductive technology: humans are produced in hatcheries and engineered into a pre-programmed caste hierarchy, with conditioning and the drug soma keeping each caste content with its place. The practitioner is the state itself through its Department of Hatcheries and Conditioning, which the film presents as the machinery of a hollow, controlled society. The sources say little about how the castes are engineered, and the film puts more weight on hedonism and consumerism than on the hatcheries. Against this system it sets natural birth: the Savages are born naturally, and the story ends with Bernard and Lenina leaving society to raise a naturally conceived child, presented as a recovery of genuine human feeling. The film therefore reads as a critique of designing people for social roles, a central fear in debates about genetic engineering and eugenics.

The story

In a future World State, people are produced in hatcheries and engineered into castes, from the decision-making Alphas to the worker Drones; exclusive relationships are obsolete and the drug soma provides instant happiness. Bernard Marx, an ambitious publicity man at the Department of Hatcheries and Conditioning, and Lenina Crowne fly over the Savage Reservation, where people are still born naturally; their aircraft crashes, and John, one of the Savages, saves them from a mob. They bring John back to the city, where he becomes a curiosity and challenges the society's values, until he falls to his death. Unlike the novel, the film ends with Lenina pregnant by Bernard after skipping her birth control; she refuses an abortion and accepts banishment. Bernard, now Director of Hatcheries, finds no comfort in soma and follows her into exile, and the last scene shows them on a beach with their naturally born daughter.
