The genetics

Genetics is at the centre of both sides of the story. The heroine, Jeannie Ferrami, is a scientist studying the heredity of personality and searching for a genetic basis of criminality by comparing identical twins raised apart, a real research design; her DNA analysis is what reveals that Steve Logan is a clone and identifies his violent double. The villains are the executives of the genetics company Genetico, who secretly created eight clones for the Pentagon as enhanced soldiers and implanted them in women without their knowledge, then try to bury the secret to protect a lucrative sale. The question of nature and nurture is explicit: many of the clones became violent criminals, which the story attributes to aggression combined with a poor upbringing, while Steve, raised in a stable family by an army officer, turns out well. The film thus sets an idealistic researcher against a corporation that misuses genetics, and suggests that genes are not destiny.

The story

In this television adaptation of Ken Follett's novel, Jeannie Ferrami is a university researcher in Philadelphia who studies the influence of genes on personality and criminality. She has developed a program that finds identical twins in national databases, even when they do not know each other, so that she can compare criminals with their twins. Her work alarms Genetico, a powerful genetics company that co-funds her university, and she is fired on false pretences. When her friend Lisa is raped by a man identical to Jeannie's new boyfriend, Steve Logan, Jeannie analyses Steve's DNA and discovers that he is one of eight clones created 27 years earlier and implanted in unsuspecting mothers. Genetico's head, Berrington Jones, made them for the Pentagon as enhanced soldiers, and the rapist is the clone he adopted as his own son. With Steve and Lisa, Jeannie stops the rapist and exposes the conspiracy before Genetico's sale to a larger company, ruining its executives.
